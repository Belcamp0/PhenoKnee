Primary TKA · cut planning

A calculator for checking that the bony resections you plan and the ones you execute agree.

Enter the leg parameters and it works out the resection depth for each cut under each alignment strategy. Enter what you measured with the caliper in theatre and it shows where the two diverge, and by how much.

It computes geometry from the figures you give it. It does not measure the knee, choose a strategy, or judge whether a plan is right for a patient.

    Against each protocol's published criteria

    The AAOS clinical practice guideline carries a strong recommendation that there is no difference in composite or functional outcomes, or in complications, between kinematic and mechanical alignment principles. No professional body recommends one strategy over another, so this page reports whether the criteria are met and what the evidence says — it does not rank the options or choose for you.
    Arithmetic comparison against published criteria, plus a summary of published findings. Not a recommendation and not a substitute for clinical judgement.

    Targets come from the plan you built. Measure each resection with a caliper and enter what you actually removed. The bone slab is thinner than the guide depth by the thickness of the saw blade.

    Confirmations

    If the trial gaps do not match

    Published corrective options for the Evolution medial-pivot system when trial gaps do not match. Reproduced as reference — the app does not select a step for you.

    The tibial resection crosshead sits at 3° of posterior slope when its vertical axis is parallel to the tibial axis. For an anatomically sloped resection, the guide is adjusted until the cutting slot matches the native slope. Resecting markedly more slope than native is the reported mechanism of posterior subsidence and posterior edge wear.
    The 4-in-1 block preparation holes give 3° of external rotation off the posterior condylar axis, or none through the 0° holes. Kinematic technique uses the 0° holes, since posterior condylar wear is usually slight. In severe varus or valgus the posterior condylar axis may be unreliable, and rotation is set against the AP axis or the epicondyles.

        What the studies show

        Trials comparing these approaches. Some found a benefit, some found none — that disagreement is the honest state of the evidence, and it is shown here rather than hidden.

        People often worry the leg will still look bowed afterwards. In one study of knees replaced this way, most patients described their leg as bowed beforehand, and every one of them described it as straight after surgery.
